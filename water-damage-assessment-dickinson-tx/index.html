Water Damage Assessment in Dickinson, TX: What to Expect

An assessment answers three questions. What is wet, how bad is the water, and what has to happen next. Here is how a professional visit works and what you should walk away with.

By the Dickinson Water Damage Pros team. Updated November 13, 2026.

Call (844) 950-6900See all services

A water damage assessment is the visit where a professional decides what is wet, how contaminated the water is and what needs to be done. It happens before drying equipment is set up, and it should end with a clear explanation of the scope and price. Understanding the steps helps you judge whether the visit was thorough.

Start with the source

The assessor asks what happened, when and what you have done since. Where the water came from matters, because a clean supply line leak, a washing machine discharge and a flooded street create very different plans. The assessor also asks how long the water has been present, since time changes both the category and the extent of damage.

Visual inspection

The first pass is visual. The assessor looks for stains, bubbling paint, warped baseboards, buckled flooring, sagging drywall and wet carpet. Smell matters too, because a musty odor can mean water has been present for a while. Any visible mold is noted, and the plan accounts for it.

Moisture meters and thermal cameras

Surfaces can look dry while the material behind them is wet, so the assessor uses tools. A moisture meter reads the water content of drywall, wood and subfloor. A thermal camera shows temperature differences that often mark damp areas. Readings are taken in rooms that seem unaffected, too, to find the edge of the wet zone and to set a dry reference for the home.

What a moisture map is

A moisture map is a record of readings across the affected area, often drawn on a floor plan. It becomes the baseline for drying and the proof of when each area reached a dry reading.

Water categories

Following the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, the assessor classifies the water. Category 1 is clean, Category 2 is contaminated and Category 3 is grossly contaminated. The category decides whether materials can be dried or have to be removed, and it can worsen over time. Our sewage backup cleanup page explains what Category 3 means in practice.

Hidden damage

Water moves. It runs under flooring, up the inside of walls and across a slab. A good assessment follows it. Baseboards may be pulled to look at the bottom of the wall, and a small hole may be made to read inside the cavity. If a ceiling is stained, the room above and the attic are checked.

The goal is to find all the wet material in one visit and not discover more halfway through the job.

What you should receive

  • A plain description of what is wet and what category the water is.
  • A scope of work that separates extraction, drying, removal and rebuilding.
  • A price explained before work starts.
  • A copy or summary of readings and photographs for your insurer.
  • A plan for equipment, monitoring visits and the expected drying period.

Red flags

Be careful with an assessment that skips the meters, that cannot explain the water category or that pushes you to sign before it has looked at the damage. A short visit that quotes a number by phone for a loss no one has seen is a poor sign.

Assessment versus a repair estimate

An assessment is about the water and the drying. A repair estimate covers rebuilding, such as new drywall, paint and flooring. They are often done in sequence, and some losses need only the first. Our hardwood floor page shows how the dry-or-replace decision works for one material.

Ask to see the readings

After the assessment, ask the technician to show you the moisture readings and the areas they mapped. Seeing the numbers helps you understand the plan and makes it easier to explain the loss to your insurer.

A Dickinson note: assessment and the 50 percent rule

FEMA's guidance for flood survivors in hazard areas urges them to consult local building officials about floodplain development permits. Nearby Kemah states that repairs costing 50 percent or more of a building's market value need building permits and elevation certificates regardless of the cause of damage.

In Dickinson, the Building Permits and Inspections office at 4403 Highway 3 is the place to ask how substantial damage is handled for your address. An assessment that records what was wet and what was removed gives you better numbers to bring to that conversation.

Sources: FEMA fact sheet on floodplain permits. City of Kemah flood protection information. City of Dickinson Building Permits and Inspections.

Dickinson assessment questions

How long does an assessment take?

Often an hour or more for a typical home, depending on how many rooms are wet. Large losses take longer.

Do I pay for an assessment?

That depends on the company and the job. Ask before the visit what is included, and ask for scope and price to be explained before work starts.

Can I do my own moisture reading?

Consumer meters exist, but readings depend on material and technique. A professional reading is more reliable.

Is an assessment the same as an inspection for insurance?

No. An insurer's adjuster decides coverage. A restoration assessment documents the water, the damage and the drying plan.
